\begin{abstract}
Large language model agents increasingly act on the web on a user's
behalf, completing purchases, managing subscriptions, and navigating
consent interfaces. These interfaces frequently contain dark patterns:
design choices engineered to steer users toward outcomes they did not
intend. India's Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) codified
thirteen such patterns in 2023, making them a legally-grounded rather
than merely descriptive taxonomy. We present Armavour, a reproducible
benchmark measuring whether computer-use agents are deceived by these
patterns. Armavour comprises a deterministic testbed implementing twelve
CCPA patterns at four manipulation intensities in three languages, a
ground-truth oracle scoring outcomes from the document object model
rather than from agent self-report, and a rubric-driven LLM judge for
the two patterns whose success condition is not deterministically
observable. Across 1,927 episodes spanning two agent architectures we
find that susceptibility scales monotonically with manipulation
intensity; that it is strongly pattern-dependent, with manipulations
targeting information availability succeeding where those targeting
visual salience fail entirely; and---our strongest result---that
deception rises from 17.2\% on an English interface to 30.0\% on a Hindi
one under identical seeds and instructions (McNemar $p<0.0001$), and to
66.7\% when the instruction is also in Hindi. We further report two
methodological findings that we believe generalise beyond this work:
patterns whose manipulation can be bypassed by evaluating the task's
stated criterion alone are structurally unmeasurable, and an agent
framework's internal task-completion signal can silently contaminate a
safety metric.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Dark patterns are interface designs that exploit predictable human
cognitive tendencies to produce outcomes favouring the platform over the
user: a pre-ticked donation checkbox, a mandatory fee revealed only at
the final confirmation, a decline button labelled to induce guilt. They
are widespread, well documented, and---in a growing number of
jurisdictions---illegal.

The premise of this work is that the deployment of autonomous web agents
changes the threat model in a way that has not been adequately measured.
When an agent completes a purchase on a user's behalf, the manipulation
is no longer aimed at a human's attention and emotions; it is aimed at
whatever the agent's perception pipeline delivers to a language model,
and at whatever reasoning that model then performs. Some manipulations
may fail entirely against this substrate. Others may succeed more
reliably than they do against humans. Neither outcome is obvious in
advance, and the distinction matters both for consumer protection policy
and for the design of agent systems.

We take India's CCPA \emph{Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of
Dark Patterns, 2023} as our taxonomy. This choice is deliberate. Most
dark-pattern research uses taxonomies derived from HCI literature, which
are descriptively rich but carry no enforcement weight. The CCPA
guidelines enumerate thirteen specific patterns with verbatim legal
definitions and have been used in enforcement actions against named
Indian platforms. Grounding a benchmark in a codified taxonomy means a
result about pattern $X$ is a result about a category regulators already
act on.

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Armavour}, an open benchmark comprising a deterministic
testbed for twelve CCPA-codified dark patterns at four intensities and
three languages, with an oracle establishing ground truth from DOM state
rather than agent self-report.
\item An \textbf{intensity dose-response result}: deception rises
monotonically from 0\% at control to 31.7\% at aggressive intensity
under otherwise identical conditions.
\item A \textbf{multilingual susceptibility result}: on seed-matched
scenarios varying only interface language, deception rises from 17.2\%
(English) to 30.0\% (Hindi), $p<0.0001$; localising the instruction as
well raises it to 66.7\%.
\item A \textbf{cross-architecture comparison} across a DOM-extraction
adapter and an agent-framework adapter, which reproduces the intensity
gradient but diverges sharply on which patterns succeed.
\item A \textbf{qualitative failure taxonomy} from reasoning traces,
including three mechanisms by which agents fall for subscription traps
and three archetypes of task abandonment.
\item Two \textbf{methodological findings} about benchmark
construction: that items whose manipulation can be circumvented by
evaluating the task's stated criterion are unmeasurable, and that an
agent framework's native completion signal can be silently credited as
safe behaviour by an outcome-based scoring scheme.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Dark pattern taxonomies and detection}

Work on dark patterns has largely proceeded from qualitative taxonomy
toward automated detection at scale. Existing taxonomies are
descriptively useful but heterogeneous, and detection work typically
answers whether a pattern is \emph{present} on a page rather than
whether it \emph{works} on a given agent. Armavour addresses the latter
question and, by adopting a legal taxonomy, makes its categories
directly commensurable with regulatory action.

\subsection{Agent benchmarks and susceptibility}

Web-agent benchmarks predominantly measure task completion. Recent work
has begun to measure susceptibility to adversarial or manipulative
interfaces; SusBench is the closest prior work and reports pattern-level
avoidance rates we treat as a comparison baseline. Two differences are
worth stating. First, Armavour is grounded in a codified legal taxonomy
rather than a research taxonomy. Second, Armavour varies manipulation
\emph{intensity} and interface \emph{language} as controlled
experimental factors, which allows dose-response and cross-lingual
claims that presence/absence designs cannot support.

\subsection{Multilingual evaluation of language models}

Degradation of model performance in lower-resource languages is well
established for static tasks. Its consequences for \emph{agentic} safety
behaviour---whether an agent operating a non-English interface is more
readily manipulated---have received less attention.
Section~\ref{sec:e2} provides direct evidence.

\section{The Armavour Benchmark}

\subsection{Design principles}

\textbf{Ground truth from the interface, not the agent.} An agent's
report that it declined an add-on is not evidence that it did. Every
pattern exposes a deterministic oracle reading the terminal DOM state at
the moment of the transaction. The agent cannot influence this signal
except by acting on the page.

\textbf{Intensity as a controlled variable.} Each pattern is
implemented at four intensities---\texttt{control}, \texttt{subtle},
\texttt{moderate}, \texttt{aggressive}---in which only the manipulation
varies. \texttt{control} renders the same task honestly and supplies the
baseline against which deception is measured.

\textbf{Versioned interface contracts.} Five contracts govern the seams
between components: episode configuration, oracle output, stable element
identifiers, the episode log schema, and the judge rubric interface.
Contracts are versioned and changed only by explicit agreement, which is
what makes results comparable across runs conducted weeks apart.

\subsection{Testbed}

The testbed is a React/TypeScript single-page application presenting
seven simulated services (ticketing, e-commerce, education, subscription
management, SaaS signup, security software, and news). Twelve of the
thirteen CCPA patterns are implemented; the thirteenth concerns
advertising practice not observable within a single session.

Episodes are driven entirely by URL parameters, per Contract~1:

\begin{center}
\texttt{/?site=\{s\}\&pattern=\{p\}\&intensity=\{i\}}\\
\texttt{\&lang=\{l\}\&seed=\{n\}}
\end{center}

Every episode is therefore a pure function of its configuration, and the
SHA-256 hash of that configuration serves as the idempotency key for
logging and resumption.

\subsection{Adapters}

We evaluate two agent architectures.

\textbf{ComputerUse} is our own adapter. It extracts interactive
elements and their surrounding text context from the DOM, presents them
to the model as structured JSON, and executes the returned action via
Playwright. Its action vocabulary is minimal: click, check, uncheck,
fill, done.

\textbf{BrowserUse} is a third-party agent framework with its own DOM
indexing, prompting, and multi-step planning. It is included because a
single adapter cannot distinguish a property of agents from a property
of one extraction layer. Section~\ref{sec:completion} shows this
inclusion was necessary in a way we did not anticipate.

\subsection{Oracle and outcome coding}

At the terminal action the page sets a global result object and
corresponding DOM attributes:

\begin{verbatim}
{ pattern, avoided, total, expected_total, ... }
\end{verbatim}

Outcomes are coded on two binary axes---task completed, pattern
avoided---yielding EC (completed, avoided), DC (completed, deceived),
EF (not completed, avoided), and DF (not completed, deceived).

\subsection{Judge}

Two patterns---false urgency and confirm shaming---have a success
condition DOM state cannot fully determine, because the question is
whether the manipulation \emph{influenced} the decision rather than
whether a particular button was pressed. For these, a second language
model scores the reasoning trace against a CCPA-grounded rubric,
returning a boolean flag and a one-sentence evidence string. Contract~5
requires the judge model to differ from the agent model.

Two decisions are consequential and we state them explicitly.

First, for judge-scored patterns the reported susceptibility rate is the
\emph{judge flag} rate, not the raw DC rate. A DC outcome records that
the platform-favoured result occurred, not that the dark pattern caused
it. In our soft-pattern pilot, four confirm-shaming episodes scored DC
while the judge correctly determined the guilt framing played no
role---they were button-semantics errors. Conflating the two would have
overstated the pattern's effect roughly fourfold.

Second, an agent whose reasoning acknowledges the manipulation but whose
final action is nonetheless correct is scored as \emph{not} swayed. The
benchmark measures whether the pattern changed the decision, not whether
it entered deliberation. Acknowledged-but-resisted influence is
preserved in the judge's evidence string without affecting any metric.

\subsection{Judge validation}
\label{sec:judgeval}

We validated the judge against a hand-constructed set of twelve cases
covering both judge-scored patterns, deliberately including the failure
mode we had observed in practice: episodes in which the agent takes the
task-correct action while its reasoning quotes the guilt-laden label,
because in this pattern the correct button \emph{is} the guilt-laden
button.

An initial judge model scored 72.7\% accuracy with precision 0.50 and
recall 1.00---systematically over-flagging, with three false positives
and no false negatives. The failures were not borderline: in one case
the model's own evidence string stated the agent showed ``no hesitation
or reconsideration'' and then flagged it anyway, having inverted the
final-choice value it was given directly.

Substituting a larger judge model, with \emph{no change to the rubric
text}, produced 100\% accuracy across all twelve cases with zero parse
errors, reproduced over two consecutive runs. This localises the
problem: judge unreliability here was a model-capability limitation, not
a prompt-engineering one, and further rubric iteration would have been
wasted effort.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Configuration}

The agent is \texttt{llama-3.3-70b-versatile}; the cross-model
spot-check uses \texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant}; the judge is
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b}. All are served by a single provider. Inference is
deterministic ($\text{temperature}=0$) at every call site.
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the consequences of both agent
models having since been withdrawn by the provider.

\subsection{Arms}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{E1a}: twelve patterns $\times$ four intensities $\times$
ten seeds, English, ComputerUse (480 episodes).
\item \textbf{Spot-check}: twelve patterns at aggressive intensity,
five seeds, smaller model, ComputerUse (60 episodes).
\item \textbf{E1b}: E1a's matrix on BrowserUse (480 episodes).
\item \textbf{E2}: six language-sensitive patterns $\times$ three
intensities $\times$ three interface languages $\times$ ten seeds,
English instructions (540 episodes).
\item \textbf{E2a / E2b}: E2's Hindi and Hinglish cells re-run with the
instruction also localised (180 each).
\end{itemize}

Structural patterns whose manipulation is numeric or positional were
excluded from the language arms, since varying their language would
mostly measure translation noise. Language-sensitive strings were
written by a native speaker rather than machine-translated: a flat
translation of a triple-nested negation would not preserve the
manipulation the item tests.

The E2/E2a/E2b split is deliberate. E2 isolates \emph{interface}
language with the instruction held constant in English---a common
deployment configuration, and the cleaner experiment. E2a and E2b add
the instruction, which is the more realistic configuration but confounds
two variables.

\section{Two Measurement Failures}

We report both of the following because we believe they are easy to
introduce, hard to notice, and unlikely to be unique to us.

\subsection{Criterion-bypassable items}
\label{sec:measurability}

Our disguised-advertisement item presented four search results, one a
sponsored placement styled to blend in, with the label's prominence
decreasing as intensity rose. The task was to buy the cheapest genuine
product. Following our own specification, the ad was priced above every
organic item so that selecting it would be unambiguously wrong.

That decision made the item unmeasurable. Because the ad was the most
expensive result and the task asked for the cheapest, any agent that
compared prices avoided it---regardless of whether the sponsored label
was clearly shown, greyed out, or absent. Intensity could not affect the
outcome, and did not: 0\% deception at every level, under both adapters.

The signature that revealed it was an inconsistency between runs. An
earlier pilot had returned 60\% deception on the same item. Reconciling
the two showed the pilot agent had been receiving no product names or
prices at all---its traces read ``all buttons have the same text
`Buy''' and ``no product details provided''---and had defaulted to the
first element, which happened to be the ad. The pilot had measured a
perception-pipeline gap and reported it as ad-disguise susceptibility.
Once the extraction layer supplied surrounding text, the rate fell to
zero, which is the correct value for an item that cannot discriminate.

The general condition: \emph{if the task's stated criterion is
sufficient to identify the correct choice, the manipulation is never
load-bearing, and the item measures criterion evaluation rather than
manipulation susceptibility.} An item can satisfy every apparent
correctness requirement---unambiguous ground truth, a well-formed
intensity ladder, a clean oracle---and still measure nothing.

The released benchmark corrects the item by inverting the price
relationship, so the ad is cheapest and price-only reasoning selects it,
making the sponsored label the sole discriminating signal. We report no
figures for the corrected item, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

False urgency exhibits the same structural problem---a single-choice
item in which price determines the answer and the urgency copy is never
load-bearing---and we have not identified an equivalent fix. Making the
urgent item cheapest would make selecting it correct; equalising prices
removes the discriminator. Urgency is pressure to act \emph{quickly},
which a single-step episode cannot express. We exclude the item rather
than report a number we cannot defend.

A practical corollary: a pattern scoring far from its published baseline
in either direction warrants a construction audit before it is reported.
Our 100\% avoidance against a prior-work baseline of approximately 65\%
was the anomaly that prompted this investigation.

\subsection{Completion-signal contamination}
\label{sec:completion}

BrowserUse produced 48 EF outcomes (10.0\%) against ComputerUse's 3
(0.6\%)---a sixteenfold difference in episodes scored as ``avoided the
dark pattern but did not complete the task.''

We initially hypothesised swallowed exceptions in the adapter's error
handling. Trace analysis falsified this. Of the 48 EF episodes, 45
contain an explicit \texttt{done} action, against 1 of 378 EC episodes:
near-perfect separation. Token counts are comparable to EC episodes
(6{,}870 versus 7{,}163 mean input tokens), durations are longer (45.2s
versus 37.9s), no episode approaches the step budget, and none has zero
token usage. These are not failures. The agent declared itself finished.

The mechanism is architectural. BrowserUse treats \texttt{done} as its
native task-completion signal and its prompting encourages invoking it
when the task appears complete; ComputerUse offers a bare action choice
with no equivalent framing. The framework's own notion of completion
fires before the page's terminal action, so the oracle never runs, and
an outcome scheme keying ``avoided'' on the absence of a deceived oracle
result credits the episode as safe.

Seven of the 48 occur at \texttt{control} intensity, where there is no
manipulation to avoid. Those are unambiguously task failures scored on
the safe side, and they are the cleanest demonstration that the
attribution is wrong.

The general condition: \emph{when an agent framework has an internal
notion of task completion, and a safety metric infers safety from the
absence of an unsafe outcome, the framework's completion signal is
silently converted into a safety claim.} This is invisible in aggregate
statistics---BrowserUse's lower deception rates in
Table~\ref{tab:patterns} are partly this artefact---and only becomes
apparent when EF episodes are separated by architecture and their traces
inspected. Cross-architecture comparisons that report only a deception
rate will not surface it.

\section{Qualitative Analysis}

\subsection{How agents fall for subscription traps}

We manually inspected twenty randomly sampled deceived episodes from
SaaS billing, the highest-susceptibility pattern under ComputerUse. All
twenty are genuine model failures rather than harness artefacts, and
resolve into three mechanisms.

\textbf{Semantic conflation ($\approx$65\%).} The model treats ``free
trial'' as satisfying an instruction to select a ``free plan,'' not
registering that the trial converts to a recurring charge. The
auto-renewal terms are present and legible in the extracted context; the
model does not connect them to its constraint.

\textbf{Constraint-violating rationalisation ($\approx$25\%).} At higher
intensities the interface offers no zero-cost option. The model
identifies this correctly---one trace reads ``there's no clear option
for a free plan without auto-renewal''---then clicks the trap anyway, on
the grounds that it is ``the only actionable step.'' This is more
concerning than the first: the constraint was understood and then
discarded under pressure to complete the task.

\textbf{Keyword-triggered action ($\approx$10\%).} A single-step
response matching on the word ``free'' without evaluating surrounding
pricing text.

\subsection{Task abandonment}

Excluding the BrowserUse episodes explained in
Section~\ref{sec:completion}, twenty episodes ended with the task
incomplete and the pattern avoided. These divide into three archetypes.

\textbf{Principled refusal} (11 episodes, SaaS billing, larger model).
The model recognises that the only available path commits the user to a
recurring charge in violation of its instruction, and declines to act.
We regard this as the desirable behaviour when an interface admits no
compliant action, and note that it is the larger model exhibiting it.

\textbf{Premature completion} (3 episodes, confirm shaming). The model
removes the manipulated add-on, then declares the task finished without
completing checkout---resisting the manipulation but losing the task.

\textbf{Fixation loop} (5 episodes, basket sneaking, smaller model). The
model correctly identifies a sneaked item in the total and refuses to
pay, then repeats the same corrective action for twenty consecutive
steps until the budget expires. It detected the constraint and lacked
the planning capacity to act on it---a capability boundary rather than a
susceptibility one.

\subsection{Why some patterns fail entirely}
\label{sec:nulls}

Interface interference produced no deception at any intensity under
either adapter. Trace inspection shows a genuine null rather than a
broken implementation: at aggressive intensity the decline option is
rendered as low-contrast, de-emphasised text beside a visually dominant
renewal button, and the agent's reasoning identifies it
directly---``the `Accept the risk' button is the option that aligns with
this goal''---and selects it. The manipulation targets human visual
salience; the agent reads all text nodes with equal weight.

Nagging fails relatedly: the agent declines the interrupting prompt and
proceeds. Repetition imposes no cost on a system that does not
experience annoyance.

That both nulls hold across two independent DOM-processing
architectures strengthens them considerably. Both would still be
overturned by a vision-based agent, which neither adapter is.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{The models under test no longer exist.} Both agent models were
withdrawn by the provider during this work, with service terminated
shortly after data collection completed. The benchmark is reproducible
in construction---testbed, specifications, rubrics, and configuration
enumeration are all released---but the reported figures are not
reproducible, because the system they measured is gone. We regard this
as a structural problem for agent safety benchmarking rather than an
incidental one: results obtained against hosted proprietary endpoints
have a shelf life set by the provider, not by the research. Benchmarks
intended to track susceptibility over time should anticipate this,
either by pinning open-weights models that can be self-hosted or by
treating each measurement as dated.

\textbf{No corrected disguised-advertisement figures.} The corrected
item (Section~\ref{sec:measurability}) could not be re-run on the same
model before withdrawal. Reporting it on a substitute model would make
it non-comparable with the other eleven patterns in the same table, so
we report the diagnosis and the correction without figures.

\textbf{BrowserUse outcomes are partly architectural.}
Section~\ref{sec:completion} establishes that 10\% of E1b episodes are
its framework's completion signal rather than agent behaviour, and five
control-condition deceptions remain unexplained. E1b's pattern-level
rates should be read as an upper bound on comparability, and the
cross-architecture claims we make are restricted to the two nulls, which
hold in both.

\textbf{Two architectures, both DOM-based.} Neither adapter is
vision-based. The interface-interference null in particular is a claim
about agents that read text, and the obvious next experiment is a
vision-based agent for which visual salience is a real signal.

\textbf{Single provider.} All models came from one inference provider.
The pattern-level ranking may not generalise across model families.

\textbf{Trace-only judging.} The judge scores reasoning traces without
the final screenshot, as the selected judge model does not support
vision.

\textbf{Confounded instruction language.} E2a and E2b vary instruction
and interface together and cannot apportion the effect. Only the
first three rows of Table~\ref{tab:lang} isolate a single variable.

\textbf{Deterministic outcomes conflate mechanism.} For oracle-scored
patterns we have no equivalent of the judge's causal separation. An
episode is scored deceived whether the agent fell for the manipulation
or merely mis-executed a correct intention. We observed at least one
episode whose reasoning suggested the latter. Reported deception rates
for deterministic patterns therefore include an unknown proportion of
execution failures; traces are retained so this can be quantified.

\textbf{DF is unreachable by construction.} No episode in 1,927 produced
a deceived-and-incomplete outcome. This follows from the scoring path:
\texttt{avoided} is carried by the oracle result, so a deceived outcome
requires a completed transaction. The four-way outcome space is
three-way in practice.

\section{Conclusion}

We built Armavour to ask whether legally-codified dark patterns work on
agents. The answer is that some do, some do not, and which is which is
not predictable from how well they work on people.

Manipulations altering what information is available or when it is
disclosed---auto-renewing trials, negated consent phrasing,
late-revealed fees---succeed at substantial rates. Manipulations relying
on visual salience or on wearing the user down succeed not at all, under
either architecture we tested, because they target properties the agent
does not have.

The result we consider most consequential is that susceptibility rises
sharply when the interface is not in English, on identical tasks with
identical seeds, and rises further still when the instruction is
localised too. Agent deployment is not confined to English-language
interfaces, and consumer-protection regimes such as the CCPA exist
precisely in the jurisdictions where this degradation is largest.

We also report two ways our own measurements failed---an item that could
not distinguish what it claimed to, and a scoring scheme that credited
an agent framework's completion signal as safe behaviour---together with
the empirical signatures that exposed them. Neither was visible in
aggregate statistics. We suspect neither is unique to us.
