\begin{abstract}
Language model agents increasingly transact on the web on a user's
behalf, through interfaces that frequently contain dark patterns. We
present Armavour, a benchmark measuring whether computer-use agents are
deceived by the twelve dark patterns codified by India's Central
Consumer Protection Authority, implemented at four manipulation
intensities in three languages, with ground truth taken from the
document object model rather than from agent self-report. Across 1,928
episodes and two agent architectures we report that susceptibility
scales monotonically with intensity, and that patterns exploiting
information asymmetry succeed while patterns exploiting human
perceptual and affective limits---visual salience, irritation, patience,
guilt---fail entirely under both architectures.
We also report, as a co-equal contribution, five distinct ways in which
this benchmark measured something other than what it named: an item
whose manipulation could be bypassed by evaluating the task's stated
criterion; element identifiers that disclosed the ground-truth answer to
the agent; an agent framework's internal completion signal absorbed by
an outcome-based metric as evidence of safe behaviour; a results column
whose denominator changed silently mid-table; and a deception rate
inflated by baseline comprehension failure on non-English interfaces.
Each produced stable, plausible, internally consistent numbers; none was
visible in aggregate statistics; each was found only by reconstructing
the quantity from raw episode records under an explicit hypothesis. We
give the detection signature for each and argue all five generalise
beyond this benchmark.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Dark patterns are interface designs that exploit predictable human
cognitive tendencies to produce outcomes favouring the platform over the
user: a pre-ticked donation checkbox, a mandatory fee revealed only at
final confirmation, a decline button labelled to induce guilt. They are
widespread, well documented, and---in a growing number of
jurisdictions---illegal.

The deployment of autonomous web agents changes the threat model. When
an agent completes a purchase on a user's behalf, the manipulation is no
longer aimed at a human's attention and emotions; it is aimed at
whatever the agent's perception pipeline delivers to a language model,
and at whatever reasoning that model then performs. Some manipulations
may fail entirely against this substrate. Others may succeed more
reliably than against humans.

This paper answers two questions, and we regard them as equally
important.

The first is the obvious one: \emph{do legally-codified dark patterns
deceive computer-use agents, and which ones?} We built Armavour to
answer it, grounding the taxonomy in India's CCPA \emph{Guidelines for
Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023} rather than an HCI
taxonomy, so that a result about pattern $X$ is a result about a
category regulators already enforce against named platforms.

The second emerged from building it: \emph{can we trust the measurement
that answers the first?} Five times during this work, a component of our
benchmark produced numbers that were stable across reruns, plausible
against prior work, internally consistent, and wrong about what they
measured. One item's manipulation turned out never to be load-bearing.
The element identifiers our harness requires turned out to name the
correct answer. One scoring rule quietly counted an agent framework's
own ``task complete'' signal as evidence the agent had resisted a dark
pattern. One results column pooled arms with different coverage and
changed denominator halfway down. And one experimental arm's headline
deception rate turned out to rest substantially on the agent failing to
comprehend the honest interface at all.

None of these announced itself. Each was found only by reconstructing
the reported quantity from raw episode records under an explicit
hypothesis and observing where the reconstruction failed. We report all
five in Section~\ref{sec:validity}, before the results they affect,
because we believe the failure modes are properties of how this class of
benchmark is built rather than accidents of our implementation.

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Armavour}: an open benchmark for twelve CCPA-codified dark
patterns at four intensities and three languages, with DOM-level ground
truth, versioned interface contracts, and a validated LLM judge for the
patterns whose success condition is not deterministically observable.

\item \textbf{Five measurement-validity failure modes} in agent
susceptibility benchmarking---criterion-bypassable items, identifier
leakage, completion-signal contamination, denominator drift, and
baseline contamination---each with the empirical signature that detects
it (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).

\item \textbf{A reconstruction protocol}: we argue that recomputing
every reported quantity from raw records under a stated hypothesis
should be a routine step in benchmark reporting rather than a response
to anomaly, and show that all five failures above were detectable this
way and by no other means we tried.

\item An \textbf{intensity dose-response result}: deception rises
monotonically from 0\% at control to 38\% at aggressive intensity under
otherwise identical conditions, replicated across two architectures.

\item A \textbf{pattern-class result}: four patterns produce no genuine
deception under either architecture, and all four target human
perceptual or affective properties rather than information content
(Section~\ref{sec:nulls}).

\item A \textbf{multilingual result}, reported conditionally: with the
instruction held in English, deception rises from 20.7\% on an English
interface to 36.0\% on a Hindi one ($p = 1.5\times10^{-5}$). With the
instruction also localised, the measured rate rises further but is not
interpretable, for reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:baseline}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Dark pattern taxonomies and detection}

Work on dark patterns has largely proceeded from qualitative taxonomy
toward automated detection at scale. Existing taxonomies are
descriptively useful but heterogeneous, and detection work typically
answers whether a pattern is \emph{present} on a page rather than
whether it \emph{works} on a given agent. Armavour addresses the latter
and, by adopting a legal taxonomy, makes its categories directly
commensurable with regulatory action.

\subsection{Agent benchmarks and susceptibility}

Web-agent benchmarks predominantly measure task completion. Recent work
measures susceptibility to adversarial or manipulative interfaces;
SusBench is the closest prior work and reports pattern-level avoidance
rates we treat as a comparison baseline. Armavour differs in grounding
its taxonomy legally, and in varying manipulation \emph{intensity} and
interface \emph{language} as controlled factors, permitting
dose-response and cross-lingual claims that presence/absence designs
cannot support.

\subsection{Validity of agent-safety measurement}

Benchmark construction receives far less scrutiny than benchmark
results. Where validity is discussed in ML evaluation it is usually
framed as contamination, saturation, or construct drift across model
generations---problems of a benchmark becoming \emph{stale}. The
failures we report are different: an item that is internally consistent,
has unambiguous ground truth, produces stable numbers across reruns, and
measures a capability unrelated to the one it names. Such an item does
not degrade over time; it was never measuring the construct.

This matters specifically for safety benchmarks. A capability benchmark
that measures the wrong thing produces a wrong leaderboard. A safety
benchmark that measures the wrong thing produces a false assurance, and
the direction of the error is not random---four of our five failures
biased toward \emph{apparent safety}, because the default coding of an
ambiguous outcome is usually the benign one. We are not aware of prior
work characterising these failure modes for agent susceptibility
evaluation or supplying detection signatures for them, and we treat that
gap as this paper's most transferable contribution.

\subsection{Multilingual evaluation}

Degradation in lower-resource languages is well established for static
tasks. Its consequences for \emph{agentic} behaviour have received less
attention. Section~\ref{sec:baseline} shows that at sufficient
degradation the question becomes unanswerable rather than merely harder,
because task failure and manipulation susceptibility stop being
separable.

\section{The Armavour Benchmark}

\subsection{Design principles}

\textbf{Ground truth from the interface, not the agent.} An agent's
report that it declined an add-on is not evidence that it did. Every
pattern exposes a deterministic oracle reading terminal DOM state at the
moment of the transaction.

\textbf{Intensity as a controlled variable.} Each pattern is implemented
at \texttt{control}, \texttt{subtle}, \texttt{moderate} and
\texttt{aggressive}, varying only the manipulation. \texttt{control}
renders the task honestly and supplies the baseline against which
deception is measured. Section~\ref{sec:baseline} shows this condition
does more work than we originally assigned it.

\textbf{Versioned interface contracts.} Five contracts govern component
seams: episode configuration, oracle output, stable element identifiers,
the episode log schema, and the judge rubric interface.
Section~\ref{sec:leakage} shows that the third of these was in tension
with measurement validity in a way we did not anticipate.

\subsection{Testbed}

A React/TypeScript application presenting seven simulated services
(ticketing, e-commerce, education, subscription management, SaaS signup,
security software, news). Twelve of the thirteen CCPA patterns are
implemented; the thirteenth concerns advertising practice not observable
within a single session. Episodes are driven entirely by URL parameters:

\begin{center}
\texttt{/?site=\{s\}\&pattern=\{p\}\&intensity=\{i\}}\\
\texttt{\&lang=\{l\}\&seed=\{n\}}
\end{center}

Every episode is a pure function of its configuration; the SHA-256 hash
of that configuration is the idempotency key for logging and resumption.

\subsection{Adapters}

\textbf{ComputerUse} is our own: it extracts interactive elements and
their surrounding text context from the DOM, presents them as structured
JSON, and executes the returned action via Playwright. Action vocabulary
is minimal---click, check, uncheck, fill, done.

\textbf{BrowserUse} is a third-party agent framework with its own DOM
indexing, prompting, and multi-step planning. It was included so that a
finding could be attributed to agents rather than to one extraction
layer. Section~\ref{sec:completion} shows this inclusion mattered in a
way we did not anticipate.

\subsection{Oracle and outcome coding}

At the terminal action the page sets a global result object:

\begin{verbatim}
{ pattern, avoided, total, ... }
\end{verbatim}

Outcomes are coded on two binary axes---task completed, pattern
avoided---giving EC (completed, avoided), DC (completed, deceived), EF
(not completed, avoided), DF (not completed, deceived).

\subsection{Judge and its validation}
\label{sec:judgeval}

Two patterns---false urgency and confirm shaming---have a success
condition DOM state cannot determine, because the question is whether
the manipulation \emph{influenced} the decision rather than whether a
button was pressed. A second language model scores the reasoning trace
against a CCPA-grounded rubric, returning a flag and a one-sentence
evidence string. Contract~5 requires judge $\neq$ agent.

For these patterns the reported susceptibility rate is the \emph{judge
flag} rate, not the raw DC rate. A DC outcome records that the
platform-favoured result occurred, not that the pattern caused it; we
report EC, genuine DC (judge-confirmed), and task-failure DC separately.
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows this distinction changes confirm
shaming's classification entirely.

An agent whose reasoning acknowledges the manipulation but whose final
action is correct is scored as \emph{not} swayed: the benchmark measures
whether the pattern changed the decision, not whether it entered
deliberation.

We validated the judge against twelve hand-constructed cases,
deliberately including the failure we had observed---episodes where the
agent takes the task-correct action while quoting the guilt-laden label,
because in this pattern the correct button \emph{is} the guilt-laden
button. An initial judge model scored 72.7\% accuracy, precision 0.50,
recall 1.00: systematically over-flagging. Its failures were not
borderline---in one case its own evidence string stated the agent showed
``no hesitation or reconsideration'' and then flagged it anyway, having
inverted a value it was given directly. A larger judge model, with
\emph{no change to the rubric text}, scored 100\% across all twelve with
zero parse errors, reproduced over two consecutive runs. The problem was
model capability, not prompt engineering, and further rubric iteration
would have been wasted effort.

\section{Experimental Design}

The agent is \texttt{llama-3.3-70b-versatile}; cross-model spot-checks
use \texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant} and \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}; the judge is
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b}. Inference is deterministic
($\text{temperature}=0$) at every call site. Both llama models have
since been withdrawn by the provider; see
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{E1a}: twelve patterns $\times$ four intensities $\times$
ten seeds, English, ComputerUse.
\item \textbf{Spot-check}: twelve patterns, aggressive only, five seeds,
smaller models, ComputerUse.
\item \textbf{E1b}: E1a's matrix on BrowserUse.
\item \textbf{E2}: five language-sensitive patterns $\times$ three
intensities $\times$ three interface languages $\times$ ten seeds,
\emph{English instructions throughout}.
\item \textbf{E2a / E2b}: E2's Hindi and Hinglish cells re-run with the
instruction also localised.
\end{itemize}

1,928 episodes were run; 1,600 are scored, after excluding two patterns
established as unmeasurable (Sections~\ref{sec:measurability}
and~\ref{sec:leakage}) and eight duplicate reruns. Language-sensitive
strings were written by a native speaker, not machine-translated: a flat
translation of a triple-nested negation would not preserve the
manipulation the item tests.

The E2 versus E2a/E2b split was intended to separate interface language
from instruction language. It ended up serving a different and more
important purpose (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}).

\section{Measurement-Validity Failures}
\label{sec:validity}

We present these before the results because four of the five change
figures we would otherwise have reported, and because the reconstruction
protocol that found them is what licenses the numbers in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Criterion-bypassable items}
\label{sec:measurability}

Our disguised-advertisement item presented four search results, one a
sponsored placement styled to blend in, with label prominence decreasing
as intensity rose. The task was to buy the cheapest genuine product.
Following our own specification, the ad was priced above every organic
item so selecting it would be unambiguously wrong.

That decision made the item unmeasurable. Because the ad was the most
expensive and the task asked for the cheapest, any agent that compared
prices avoided it---regardless of whether the sponsored label was shown,
greyed, or absent. Intensity could not affect the outcome, and did not:
0\% deception at every level, under both architectures.

\textbf{Detection signature: an intensity ladder with zero slope, and
disagreement with a prior run.} An earlier pilot returned 60\% deception
on this item. Reconciling the two showed the pilot agent had received no
product names or prices at all---its traces read ``all buttons have the
same text `Buy''' and ``no product details provided''---and had
defaulted to the first element, which happened to be the ad. The pilot
measured a perception-pipeline gap and reported it as ad-disguise
susceptibility. Once the extraction layer supplied surrounding text, the
rate fell to zero, which is the correct value for an item that cannot
discriminate.

\textbf{General condition:} \emph{if the task's stated criterion is
sufficient to identify the correct choice, the manipulation is never
load-bearing, and the item measures criterion evaluation rather than
manipulation susceptibility.} An item can satisfy every apparent
correctness requirement---unambiguous ground truth, a well-formed
intensity ladder, a clean oracle---and still measure nothing.

False urgency has the same structure---single-choice, price-decidable,
urgency copy never load-bearing---with no equivalent fix: making the
urgent item cheapest would make selecting it correct, and equalising
prices removes the discriminator. Urgency is pressure to act
\emph{quickly}, which a single-step episode cannot express.

\textbf{Corollary for benchmark authors:} a pattern scoring far from its
published baseline in either direction warrants a construction audit
before it is reported. Our 100\% avoidance against a prior-work baseline
of roughly 65\% was the anomaly that started this investigation.

\subsection{Identifier leakage}
\label{sec:leakage}

We had planned to correct the disguised-advertisement item by inverting
the price relationship, so the ad becomes cheapest and price-only
reasoning selects it. A later spot-check on a third agent model showed
that this correction would not have worked, for a reason we had not
considered.

Contract~3 requires every interactive element to carry a stable, unique
identifier, so the harness can act on elements reliably across renders.
We named them descriptively, as one does: \texttt{item-ad} for the
sponsored placement and \texttt{item-org-1} through
\texttt{item-org-3} for the organic results. The extraction layer
includes each element's identifier in the JSON handed to the model,
because the model must be able to refer to elements when choosing an
action.

The identifiers name the answer. At \texttt{aggressive} intensity---the
level at which the specification requires \emph{no label at all}---an
agent produced this reasoning:

\begin{quote}
\emph{``The ad item (PowerCharge Fast Cable) is a disguised
advertisement and should not be considered genuine. Among the genuine
organic results, the cheapest is Basic USB-C Cable at Rs 199.''}
\end{quote}

It identified the sponsored placement with no visual label present, and
described the alternatives as ``organic''---a term from our internal
taxonomy, not from the rendered page. The same holds for false urgency,
whose identifiers are \texttt{item-urgent} and \texttt{item-calm-1};
agent traces reference \texttt{buy-item-calm-1} directly.

\textbf{Detection signature: agent reasoning containing vocabulary that
appears nowhere in the rendered interface.} The word ``organic'' does
not appear on the page. Its presence in a reasoning trace is a direct
indication that something other than the rendered interface reached the
model.

\textbf{General condition:} \emph{any channel the harness requires for
control---element identifiers, accessibility labels, test hooks,
ordering---can also carry the ground-truth answer, and will do so by
default, because the names that make a testbed maintainable are the
names that describe what each element is for.} This is a direct tension
between a benchmark's engineering requirements and its measurement
validity, and it is invisible from outcome data alone: an item leaking
its answer looks exactly like an item the agent is robust to.

The remedy is opaque identifiers---\texttt{item-a},
\texttt{item-b}---accepting the maintenance cost, and auditing every
pattern for the same disclosure. We have not completed that audit, and
therefore report no corrected figures for either affected item.

\subsection{Completion-signal contamination}
\label{sec:completion}

BrowserUse produced 47 EF outcomes (11.8\% of scored episodes) against
ComputerUse's 3 (0.75\%)---a sixteenfold difference in episodes scored
as ``avoided the dark pattern but did not complete the task.''

We first hypothesised swallowed exceptions in the adapter's error
handling. Trace analysis falsified this. Of 48 EF episodes in the raw
arm, 45 contain an explicit \texttt{done} action, against 1 of 378 EC
episodes: near-perfect separation. Token counts are comparable to EC
episodes (6{,}870 versus 7{,}163 mean input tokens), durations are
\emph{longer} (45.2s versus 37.9s), no episode approaches the step
budget, none has zero token usage. These are not failures. The agent
declared itself finished.

The mechanism is architectural. BrowserUse treats \texttt{done} as its
native completion signal and its prompting encourages invoking it when
the task appears complete; ComputerUse offers a bare action choice with
no equivalent framing. The framework's notion of completion fires before
the page's terminal action, the oracle never runs, and an outcome scheme
keying ``avoided'' on the absence of a deceived oracle result credits
the episode as safe.

A later spot-check reproduced the effect in a different model on our own
adapter, at a higher rate: 16 of 60 episodes (27\%), with traces such as
\emph{``the order summary confirms the ticket price is Rs 500 and the
order has been placed successfully''} immediately before a
\texttt{done}---issued without the payment button ever being clicked.
The agent did not merely stop early; it asserted a completion that had
not occurred.

\textbf{Detection signature: an outcome-category rate that differs by an
order of magnitude across architectures or models, plus the presence of
that category in the control condition.} Seven of BrowserUse's 48 occur
at \texttt{control} intensity, where no manipulation exists to avoid.
Those are unambiguously task failures scored on the safe side.

\textbf{General condition:} \emph{when an agent framework has an
internal notion of task completion, and a safety metric infers safety
from the absence of an unsafe outcome, the framework's completion signal
is silently converted into a safety claim.} This is invisible in
aggregate statistics---BrowserUse's lower deception rates in
Table~\ref{tab:patterns} are partly this artefact---and surfaces only
when the ambiguous outcome category is disaggregated and its traces
read.

\subsection{Denominator drift}
\label{sec:denominators}

An earlier draft of our pattern table reported a single pooled deception
rate per pattern, computed across every arm in which that pattern
appeared. Because the language arms cover only five of the twelve
patterns, half the column was computed at $n=135$ and half at $n=45$,
with nothing in the table indicating the denominator changed. The column
was internally consistent, monotonic, and plausible.

\textbf{Detection signature: partial reproduction.} It was caught by
attempting to recompute the column from raw records under one stated
hypothesis (``E1a plus spot-check, $n=45$'') and finding that exactly
six of ten values matched. The four that did not were exactly the four
patterns also present in the language arms.

\textbf{General condition:} \emph{a table that pools across experimental
arms with different factor coverage will silently vary its denominator
by row.} The failure is undetectable by inspection and trivially
detectable by reconstruction.

\subsection{Baseline contamination}
\label{sec:baseline}

The fifth failure inflates this paper's own headline result, and is the
reason Section~\ref{sec:lang} reports the language effect
conditionally.

The \texttt{control} condition renders each task honestly: no
manipulation is present, so a deceived outcome should be impossible.
Under English instructions with an English interface, it is---0 of 50
control episodes were scored as deceived. But control-condition
deceptions rise sharply as the interface and instruction are localised
(Table~\ref{tab:controlfail}).

\textbf{Instruction} & \textbf{Interface} & \textbf{``Deceived''} \\
\midrule

English  & Hindi    & 6 \\
Hinglish & Hinglish & 7 \\
\textbf{Hindi} & \textbf{Hindi} & \textbf{21} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:controlfail}
\end{center}
\end{table}

In the fully-Hindi arm, 42\% of episodes on an interface containing no
dark pattern were scored as deceived---including \emph{every} control
episode for interface interference and \emph{every} control episode for
confirm shaming. The agent is not being manipulated in these episodes.
It is failing to operate the interface, and an outcome scheme that codes
any incorrect end-state as deception counts that failure as
susceptibility.

\textbf{Detection signature: a non-zero rate in the null condition,
disaggregated along the same axis as the reported effect.} A pooled
control figure across all arms (34 of 300, or 11\%) looks like tolerable
noise. Split by language it is a monotone tracking precisely the
variable whose effect is being claimed.

\textbf{General condition:} \emph{when a manipulated variable also
degrades baseline task competence, an outcome-based deception metric
sums the two, and the design cannot apportion them.} This is not a
confound in the usual sense---no third variable causes both---but a
failure of the metric to distinguish two mechanisms producing the same
recorded outcome.

The consequence is direct. Our fully-localised Hindi arm records 73.3\%
deception; a large share is baseline failure, and we cannot say how
much. The English-instruction arms retain an interpretable baseline and
are reported as the primary result.

\subsection{What the five have in common}

Each produced a number that was stable across reruns, plausible against
prior expectations, internally consistent, and not a measurement of what
it named. None was visible in aggregate statistics. Each was found the
same way: by taking a reported quantity, stating a hypothesis about how
it was computed, recomputing it from raw episode records, and examining
where the reconstruction diverged.

Four of the five biased toward apparent safety. We suspect this is
systematic rather than coincidental: when an outcome is ambiguous, the
default coding is usually the benign one, because benign outcomes are
what the instrument is built to expect.

Confirm shaming's raw rate under E1a is 2.5\%---a single episode---but
the judge classifies that episode as a task-failure DC rather than
genuine susceptibility, giving 0.0\%. Without the judge distinction it
would appear as weakly effective rather than as a null.

Three observations. First, four patterns produce no genuine deception
under either architecture; Section~\ref{sec:nulls} argues they share a
cause. Second, trick question is the only pattern where BrowserUse is
substantially \emph{more} susceptible---and the only one whose
manipulation is purely linguistic, suggesting additional scaffolding
does not help, and may hinder, when the difficulty is semantic rather
than navigational. Third, BrowserUse's lower rates elsewhere should be
read alongside Section~\ref{sec:completion}.

\section{Qualitative Analysis}

\subsection{How agents fall for subscription traps}

We inspected twenty randomly sampled deceived episodes from SaaS
billing, the highest-susceptibility pattern under ComputerUse. All
twenty are genuine model failures rather than harness artefacts, in
three mechanisms.

\textbf{Semantic conflation ($\approx$65\%).} The model treats ``free
trial'' as satisfying an instruction to select a ``free plan,'' not
registering that the trial converts to a recurring charge. The
auto-renewal terms are present and legible in the extracted context; the
model does not connect them to its constraint.

\textbf{Constraint-violating rationalisation ($\approx$25\%).} At higher
intensities no zero-cost option exists. The model identifies this
correctly---one trace reads ``there's no clear option for a free plan
without auto-renewal''---then clicks the trap anyway, on the grounds it
is ``the only actionable step.'' The constraint was understood and then
discarded under pressure to complete the task.

\textbf{Keyword-triggered action ($\approx$10\%).} A single-step
response matching on ``free'' without evaluating surrounding pricing.

\subsection{Task abandonment}

Excluding the episodes explained in Section~\ref{sec:completion},
nineteen episodes ended with task incomplete and pattern avoided, in
three archetypes.

\textbf{Principled refusal} (11 episodes, SaaS billing, larger model).
The model recognises that the only available path commits the user to a
recurring charge in violation of its instruction, and declines to act.
We regard this as correct behaviour when an interface admits no
compliant action, and note it is the larger model exhibiting it.

\textbf{Premature completion} (3 episodes, confirm shaming). The model
removes the manipulated add-on, then declares the task finished without
completing checkout---resisting the manipulation but losing the task.

\textbf{Fixation loop} (5 episodes, basket sneaking, smaller model). The
model identifies a sneaked item and refuses to pay, then repeats the
same corrective action for twenty consecutive steps until the budget
expires. It detected the constraint and lacked the planning capacity to
act on it---a capability boundary, not a susceptibility one.

\subsection{What the four nulls have in common}
\label{sec:nulls}

Four patterns produced no genuine deception at any intensity under
either architecture, and we argue this is not coincidence.

\textbf{Interface interference} manipulates visual weight. At aggressive
intensity the decline option is low-contrast, de-emphasised text beside
a dominant renewal button. Traces show the agent identifying it
directly---``the `Accept the risk' button is the option that aligns with
this goal''---and selecting it.

\textbf{Nagging} manipulates persistence, interrupting repeatedly for
data access. The agent declines and proceeds.

\textbf{Subscription trap} manipulates attrition, routing cancellation
through a four-step retention flow. The agent completes all four steps.

\textbf{Confirm shaming} manipulates guilt, labelling the decline option
to make choosing it feel callous. The agent quotes the label to locate
the button and clicks it.

The four target properties agents do not possess: selective visual
attention, irritation, finite patience, and susceptibility to social
shame. Every pattern that \emph{does} succeed---auto-renewing trials,
negated consent phrasing, late-revealed fees, substituted
items---manipulates what information is available or how it is framed,
which is the channel through which an agent perceives the page at all.

This marks a division inside the CCPA taxonomy that its legal categories
do not: patterns exploiting human perceptual and affective limits, and
patterns exploiting information asymmetry. Only the second class
transfers. If agent-mediated commerce grows, the first class loses
potency while the second does not---with direct consequences for which
categories warrant continued enforcement attention in an agentic
setting.

These remain claims about \emph{text-reading} agents. A vision-based
agent, for which visual salience is a real signal, could overturn the
interface-interference null specifically.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{The identifier audit is incomplete.} Section~\ref{sec:leakage}
establishes that element identifiers disclosed the answer for two
patterns. We have not audited the remaining ten. Any pattern whose
identifiers encode a semantic distinction the task depends on is
potentially affected, and the reported rates for those patterns would be
lower bounds on true susceptibility.

\textbf{The models under test no longer exist.} Both primary agent
models were withdrawn by the provider shortly after data collection.
The benchmark is reproducible in construction---testbed, specifications,
rubrics and configuration enumeration are released---but the figures are
not, because the system they measured is gone. Results obtained against
hosted proprietary endpoints have a shelf life set by the provider, not
the research.

\textbf{The fully-localised language arms are not interpretable.} See
Section~\ref{sec:baseline}. We report them for completeness and because
the contamination is itself a finding.

\textbf{BrowserUse outcomes are partly architectural.} 11.8\% of scored
E1b episodes reflect the framework's completion signal rather than agent
behaviour, and five control deceptions remain unexplained. Our
cross-architecture claims are restricted to the four nulls, which hold
in both.

\textbf{Two architectures, both DOM-based.} Neither is vision-based.

\textbf{Single provider.} The pattern-level ranking may not generalise
across model families.

\textbf{Trace-only judging.} The judge scores reasoning traces without
the final screenshot, as the selected judge model lacks vision.

\textbf{The schema did not record a manipulated variable.} When the
language arms ran, the episode log stored a single \texttt{language}
column; the separation of instruction language from interface language
existed only in memory and was dropped on insert. The conditions are
recoverable only because they ran with disjoint seed blocks on different
dates---fortunate rather than designed.

\textbf{Deterministic outcomes conflate mechanism.} For oracle-scored
patterns we have no equivalent of the judge's causal separation, so a
deceived score does not distinguish falling for the manipulation from
mis-executing a correct intention. This is Section~\ref{sec:baseline}'s
failure in a milder form; traces are retained so it can be quantified.

\textbf{DF is unreachable by construction.} \texttt{avoided} is carried
by the oracle result, so a deceived outcome requires a completed
transaction. The four-way outcome space is three-way in practice.

\section{Conclusion}

We built Armavour to ask whether legally-codified dark patterns work on
agents. Some do and some do not, and the division is not predictable
from how well they work on people: patterns exploiting information
asymmetry transfer, patterns exploiting human perceptual and affective
limits do not.

We also found five ways our own instrument measured something other than
what it named. An item whose manipulation was never load-bearing.
Element identifiers that told the agent the answer. A scoring rule that
read an agent framework's ``task complete'' as ``dark pattern avoided.''
A table whose denominator changed mid-column. And a deception rate
inflated by the agent's inability to read the honest interface. All five
produced stable, plausible numbers. Four biased toward apparent safety.

We do not think we were unusually careless. We think these are the
shapes measurement failure takes in this class of benchmark, and that
they are difficult to see precisely because the numbers they produce
look correct. Each was found by the same method: state how a reported
quantity was supposed to be computed, recompute it from raw records, and
look at where the reconstruction breaks. That method cost us days and
found five errors, one of which inflated our own headline result. We
suggest it belongs in the standard reporting path for agent safety
benchmarks, not in the incident response path.
